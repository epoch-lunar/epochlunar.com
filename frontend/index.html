Open-source lunar timekeeping

Precision time, carried onboard.

There is no GPS at the Moon. The Space Time Card is a plug-and-play time appliance for spacecraft: an ensemble of precision oscillators, cross-disciplined by consensus, presented to the host as a standard PTP Leader over Ethernet. Trusted time without waiting for a constellation.

01 — The instrument

One cable. One protocol. The host stays ignorant.

PTP Leader over Ethernet

The card joins the spacecraft network as a Precision Time Protocol Leader. Any PTP Follower consumes hardware-timestamped packets — the same stack that synchronizes datacenters to sub-microsecond precision. No PCIe, no custom drivers.

Oscillator ensemble

A single chip-scale atomic clock drifts by hundreds of microseconds within days. Combining oscillators with Allan-deviation-based weighting suppresses short-term phase noise and constrains long-term drift. If one fails, the ensemble excludes it without losing time continuity.

Relativity onboard

The card models the ~56 µs/day rate offset caused by the Moon's gravitational potential in real time, on flight-class processors — not confined to ground systems.

Upgradeable in flight

Synchronization logic lives in software on the SoC; timing algorithms live in the FPGA. A mission that launches with basic PTP disciplining can adopt ensemble averaging years later — just an upload. Oscillators sit on a swappable daughter board.

02 — Adoption

Buy it, blend it, or brew it yourself.

Open interfaces mean teams choose how they adopt — trading dollars for effort at whatever rate fits their budget and schedule. The interface stays stable in every case, so systems built independently remain compatible.

MODE 02

Blend

Swap a different oscillator onto the daughter board, or run modified firmware on the same hardware — new configurations without fragmenting the interfaces.

Brew

03 — Where it stands

A new epoch starts now.

The underlying physics and synchronization techniques are well understood. The remaining challenge is implementation at scale — hardware characterization, ensemble algorithms, embedded lunar frame transforms, test procedures, validation data. A university team that characterizes oscillator performance in thermal vacuum produces data that benefits every future integrator.

Space Time Card V1 was designed, built, and tested by undergraduate students at Rochester Institute of Technology. Flight-representative prototypes are in work for 2026; thermal vacuum and vibration testing begins 2027. Epoch is developed under the Open Lunar Foundation.
